1. Why expression scoring was not enough

The first study measured how strongly each handoff text expressed the stated value, with an automated rater. A rater-free text-similarity check found that texts written with the priority became more similar to the no-priority texts across the handoffs. That shows the texts converged, which supports a real change in what they carried; it does not measure value expression, so it is not an independent validation that expression of the value declined. Separately, a text can discuss a value without acting on it, and recommend in line with a priority without naming it. A score for expression cannot say which option the advice, or a later decision, would favour. That is why the later studies record choices.

2. The restatement problem

After the third handoff, most of the remaining measured expression sits in texts that explicitly restate the user's priority. The rater cannot tell restating a priority from being guided by it. The same caution applies to Study 3B's value-name highlight: in this pilot, naming the prioritized value in the final handoff did not reliably go with choosing by it (– of 28 Delegated handoffs named it). Naming a value does not show it influenced the choice, and not naming it does not show it was lost.

3. Why the first delegation follow-up was stopped

Before Study 2 was run, its plan fixed criteria that a delegation follow-up on the same decisions had to meet: the priority had to change single-turn choices reliably, and at least 8 decisions needed both perfect paired sensitivity and a stable no-priority choice, so that a later drift back toward that default could be detected. Only – qualified, mostly because no-priority answers were UNCLEAR or varied, and single-turn accuracy was at ceiling. The follow-up therefore did not proceed under its own rules. A separately designed Study 3 was frozen and then, by the investigator's decision, not run. Study 3B was designed afterwards as a smaller development pilot with its own frozen plan.

4. Why Study 3B is a seven-scenario development pilot

It was meant to test whether the full delegation design works end to end (chains, branches, parsing, ledger, cost) and to give a first look at choices, within a $15 budget on one model. The seven decisions were previously used in Study 2, so they are not fresh stimuli. It is reported descriptively, with all seven scenarios shown whatever their outcome. Before the results were opened, a written declaration recorded that no confirmatory study is planned or authorized and that any future one would be frozen independently of these results.

5. Why UNCLEAR is different from choosing against the priority

An UNCLEAR answer declines to choose. Many recorded UNCLEAR answers say the advice they received was a framework rather than a recommendation, or that the decision depends on preferences they were not told. Choosing the opposite option is a different outcome: a choice was made, and it went the other way. The site always shows the two separately and never counts UNCLEAR as a match. Whether UNCLEAR is appropriate in a given case is a judgment this study does not make.

6. A final reminder versus T4 regeneration

Both bring the priority back, in different places. The final reminder (Restored) adds one line to the decision prompt and keeps the handoff text byte-identical, so the two prompts differ only by that line. In these recorded runs it shows whether supplying the original priority again at the final decision, over the identical handoff 6, was followed by a different choice. With one sampled answer per condition, it cannot show that a difference is reliable, or why it occurred. It does not repair or rewrite the chain. T4 re-anchoring regenerates the fourth handoff from the same saved first three, with the priority inserted, and lets the rest of the chain run; the decision gets no reminder. Its T4–T6 are new samples, so its difference from Delegated mixes the inserted priority with ordinary run-to-run variation. In this pilot the reminder gave – of 28 matches and re-anchoring –; some cases that matched without re-anchoring did not match after it (see the counterexample).

7. Incomplete human validation

A human-rating study of the expression measure was designed and its materials built, but no ratings have been collected. As designed, it could not test the six-handoff decline directly: it had no first-handoff items and rated the stated value only. Until a human comparison is run, Study 1's scores remain readings from an automated instrument, labelled provisional.

8. A measured deviation is not an unauthorized one

The Auditor records observations: a final choice that differs from the option built to match a stated priority, or a value that is expressed less. Whether such a departure is a problem depends on context the Auditor does not have: the user may have changed their mind, the advice may have raised something they would endorse, or the priority may not fit the decision. An earlier drift classifier was never validated and is not used to claim violations. In the audit tool, the visitor, not the software, decides whether a departure needs review.

Scope, in one place
  • One model (Gemini 2.5 Pro), one workflow shape, English text.
  • Constructed decisions with stipulated priorities; no real users.
  • Study 1 and Study 3B use different scenarios and are not one causal experiment.
  • No claim about model intentions, memory, misalignment, or benefits and harms to people.
