Research

Research and evidence

The full research behind the tool: one primary study (Study 4), two exploratory supporting studies, the earlier pilots that led to them, their methods and limits, and the data. Nothing here is needed to use the tool.

Primary study: Study 4

After six AI handoffs, decisions became much less sensitive to the user's stated priority and resembled decisions made without an explicit priority. Restating the priority at the final step restored sensitivity in this controlled experiment.

Study 4 used Gemini 2.5 Pro on 12 constructed scenarios (one template family), each under 2 opposite priorities and in 2 option orders, with 2 independent handoff chains per case. A unit counts as paired-sensitive only if both opposite priorities got their own option. Sensitive units: told directly 24 of 24, after six handoffs 6 of 48, with the priority restated at the final step 47 of 48. Single decisions: 48 of 48, 27 of 96 (35 opposite, 34 declined) and 95 of 96. After six handoffs the model declined in 35% of decisions, the same range as with no priority at all (33% deciding directly, 38% after a six-handoff chain without a priority). These results are descriptive, not causal; they concern one model and one family of constructed scenarios, and what the handoff texts contained was not analysed.

How the evidence fits together

Study 4 is the primary evidence. Before it, three earlier studies were run in the order Study 1, Study 2, Study 3B, each answering one part of the question. Study 1 used different decisions from Studies 2 and 3B, and Study 3B reused seven Study 2 decisions with a different procedure. Study 3C then re-examined the 28 saved Study 3B cases after each handoff, and the three-model comparison used published dilemmas. Study 4 used its own 12 constructed scenarios. These are separate pieces of evidence, not one combined experiment, and they are never pooled. In each, the user's priority is written into the prompt in plain words; the question is what happens to that priority afterwards.

What this does not establish

  • Not evidence that the model forgot the priority. The results concern final decisions. What the handoff texts contained was not analysed in Study 4, so they cannot say whether the priority was lost from the text or present but unused. In the earlier pilot, many handoffs still referred to the user's goal while arguing for a different framing.
  • Not causal, and not a general estimate. Study 4 has 12 constructed scenarios from one template family and one model; with about 12 scenario clusters its intervals are descriptive. The earlier pilot has seven decisions. The results describe these cases, not arbitrary external systems.
  • Restating is instruction-following, not better decisions. Matching a restated priority shows the decision-maker follows an instruction placed in front of it.
  • No claim about real people. Nothing here measures benefit or harm to actual users, or violations of anyone's autonomy.
  • Study 1 is not a measure of decisions, and its automated rater has not been validated against human judgment.
More detail: further caveats
  • UNCLEAR is not the same as choosing against the priority. It is a declined choice; it is counted separately and never as a match.
  • Mentioning is not following. A handoff can name the prioritized value and still lead to the opposite choice, and the reverse. The value-name highlight is a literal word match, not a semantic classifier or a validated predictor.
  • One model, six roles. In Study 4 and the earlier studies, all handoffs and decisions are separate calls to Gemini 2.5 Pro with different role prompts, not different models. The three-model comparison is exploratory, uses different items and is never pooled with them.
  • T4 re-anchoring regenerates text. Its T4–T6 are new samples, so its difference from Delegated mixes the effect of the inserted priority with ordinary run-to-run variation.
  • In the pilot, Direct and Restored were at ceiling (28 of 28). A ceiling leaves no room to see variation within those conditions.
  • Development pilot. The seven decisions were also used in Study 2. Study 3B is an exploratory development pilot; Study 4 was run afterwards, on its own 12 scenarios, as the primary study.
  • Study 1 is not a general estimate. It covers eight values, one workplace-advice workflow, 45 scenarios and one model.
  • Study 1's scale has a ceiling. At T1, 357 of the 360 texts written with a priority scored +2, so some differences are 0 by construction.
  • Study 2 is an easy test by design. Near-perfect accuracy there says little about harder, realistic decisions.
  • Displayed Study 1 text is lightly redacted. Questionnaire-derived priority wording is withheld and shown as a marked redaction. Study 2 and Study 3B priorities were written for those studies and are shown in full.
  • Examples are chosen by fixed rules from IDs or, for the walkthrough, from a named subset; they are not chosen to be typical.

History: why the first delegation follow-up was stopped

Before any data were collected, the study plan fixed the criteria a delegation follow-up had to meet.

Study 3B was designed afterwards as a separate, smaller development pilot, with its own frozen plan, sealed outcomes and an independent recomputation. See Design decisions.

Step 5 · Study 3B

What happens when the priority is restored?

The two interventions are different. The final reminder adds the priority to the decision prompt and leaves the handoffs unchanged. T4 re-anchoring regenerates the fourth handoff with the priority, then lets T5 and T6 run as usual, and adds no reminder at the decision. See the recorded example · see the counterexample.

Study 2 · one decision, one turn

Does a stated priority change the model's choice?

The model chose between two options in 35 decisions, with and without a stated priority. Pick one to read its answers.

More detail: statistics, hypotheses, method

Each decision was asked with no priority (three repetitions) and once under each opposing priority, always in both option orders: 350 recorded answers. Whether a choice matches a priority follows from how the decision was built; no AI rater is involved. Study 1 measures how strongly text expresses a value, not which option it recommends; this study measures the choice.

Scope. The single-turn behavioral study does not establish priority loss during six-turn delegation and is not a direct validation of the original drift classifier. It tests whether a priority stated in a single prompt changes a single choice.

Study 1: does a stated priority stay visible as advice is passed along?The provisional rater result and one workflow stage by stage

Study 1 · six handoffs

Does a stated priority stay visible as advice is passed along?

Compared with the same tasks without a stated priority, an automated rater found much stronger expression of that priority in the first response than in the sixth.

Automated rater · provisional. Not yet checked against human judgment. It measures expression in text, not decisions.

This matched with-versus-without comparison was specified after the preregistered analysis, whose hypotheses used a different comparison. Treat it as a post-hoc estimate.

More detail: what was measured, intervals

What was measured: how strongly each text expressed the stated value, scored from −1 (opposed) to +2 (dominant). It does not measure whether the priority changed the advice's final recommendation. Zero means no difference in this score; it does not mean the texts or their recommendations were identical.

A user tells an AI advisor what matters most to them. The advice then passes through a critic and a summarizer, and the cycle repeats. Only the first agent explicitly receives the priority; later steps can still discuss or paraphrase it. The decrease was substantial but not uniform. Expression rebounded between T2 and T3 before declining again. Average over 45 dilemmas × 8 values = 360 workflows, each compared with three matched runs that had no stated priority.

Measurement status: provisional

These scores are readings from an automated rater (Gemini 2.5 Pro applying a fixed Schwartz-values rubric). The rater gives stable scores and resisted rewording on short single-author passages, but it has not been validated against human judgment on multi-agent text. The decline is therefore evidence about measured value expression, not about decisions.

    The scores behind the difference

    More detail: restating vs being guided

    The rater scores how central a value is in a text. A summary that repeats “you said this matters most to you” can score high even if its advice would be the same without the priority. Advice that quietly follows the priority without naming it can score low. The rater cannot tell these apart, so these scores cannot establish that the priority substantively shaped the advice.

    Stages whose text explicitly restates the user's priority are flagged, and the matching phrases are highlighted. This is an automatic pattern match on the displayed text, not a human reading. It can miss paraphrases and catch incidental phrases.
